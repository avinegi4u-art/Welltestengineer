Key Design Considerations

  • Limestone reacts ~5–10x faster than dolomite at the same temperature and HCl concentration. Plan pump rates accordingly to stay in the wormholing regime.
  • For dolomite, higher HCl concentrations (23–28%) are often preferred since the slower reaction gives more penetration depth before spending.
  • In mixed lithology zones, design for the faster-reacting component (limestone) to avoid face dissolution and ensure deeper penetration.
  • Bottomhole temperature is the strongest control on reaction rate. Above 250°F, consider retarded acid systems or organic acids for both lithologies.
  • CO₂ evolution: dolomite produces 2 mol CO₂ per mol acid spent vs. 1 for limestone — plan for higher gas volumes during flowback.
Operational Notes & Selection Guide

OPF (Open Perforation Flow) vs OPR (Open Perforation Rate)

  • OPF — Open-ended flow through perforations with no restriction. Acid enters the formation by gravity and wellbore hydrostatic overbalance. Best for: acid washes, low-rate soaks, and cleanout operations where deep penetration is not the goal.
  • OPR — Controlled injection rate through perforations, typically 0.25–1.0 BPM/ft. The pump rate is the critical parameter. Best for: matrix acidizing aimed at wormhole creation, where exceeding the optimal injection rate means wasting acid on face dissolution.
  • Selection Rule: If the goal is to remove near-wellbore damage (scale, mud cake, completion debris), OPF with a soak period is often sufficient. If the goal is to create new flow channels (wormholes) past the damage zone, use OPR with a designed pump schedule.
  • Practical tip: On coil tubing operations, OPR allows better control of acid placement across the interval. Spot acid at the target depth, then pump at the designed rate using the CT as a conduit.

Acid Wash vs Bullheading

  • Acid Wash — Circulate acid across the target interval using CT or tubing. Acid contacts the formation face while returns come up the annulus. Provides better control of placement and contact time. Preferred when you need precise acid placement across specific zones, especially in long horizontal sections.
  • Bullheading — Pump acid down the tubing or casing with no returns; all fluid enters the formation. Simpler operationally but less control over which zones receive acid. The lowest-pressure zone takes most of the fluid.
  • When to use Acid Wash: Wellbore cleanout, scale removal from perforations, remove filter-cake or completion fluid residue. Typically done at low rates (0.5–2 BPM) with 5–15% HCl. Soak time 30–60 min, then circulate out.
  • When to use Bullheading: Matrix stimulation where the goal is to push acid into the formation. Higher rates and pressures. Works well in wells with single producing zones. Poor zonal coverage in multi-zone completions unless combined with diverters.
  • Caution: Never bullhead into a well with uncertain tubular integrity. Pressure test tubing and casing before bullheading. Ensure wellhead pressure ratings accommodate the maximum pump pressure + hydrostatic + friction loss.
  • Diversion: For both methods in multi-zone completions, consider mechanical (ball sealers, packers) or chemical diversion (VDA, self-diverting acid, fiber) to ensure even acid distribution.

Pump Rate Guidelines for Matrix Acidizing

  • Below fracture pressure: Always verify the fracture gradient. Matrix treatment = injection below fracture pressure. Exceeding it wastes acid into fractures with no wormhole benefit.
  • Optimal injection rate (OIR): For limestone, typically 0.25–0.5 BPM/ft of interval. For dolomite, can go higher (0.5–1.0 BPM/ft) since the reaction is slower.
  • Surface pressure monitoring: Track real-time pressure response. A declining surface pressure at constant rate = successful wormhole propagation. Increasing pressure = possible screen-out or emulsion block.
  • Temperature effect: At higher BHT, reduce concentration or add retarder to maintain acid penetration depth. Spending time drops exponentially with temperature.
